TAKE A SEAT

AN INVITATION TO SIT

Public seating begins with a simple invitation. A bench, chair or ledge offers someone a place to stop moving and spend time in the city. These objects can make a busy sidewalk feel more welcoming because they allow the body to pause without needing to buy anything.

The invitation is shaped by location, material and comfort. A seat facing the street creates a different experience from one hidden beside a building. Some seats encourage conversation while others separate people into individual spaces.

These observations look at seating before its rules become obvious. The surfaces appear public and available, but their shapes already begin to suggest where someone should sit and how the surrounding space should be used.

GUIDING THE BODY

A chair does more than provide a surface. The height of the seat, the angle of the back and the position of each armrest tell the body how to behave. These instructions usually feel ordinary because they are built into objects people use every day.

Dividers create a clear position for each person. Narrow surfaces encourage people to sit upright and leave after a short break. Sloped structures and leaning bars support waiting, but they make deeper rest difficult.

This type of design does not need a written rule. The furniture communicates through comfort and discomfort. People adjust their posture, distance and length of stay according to the limits of the object.

RESTRICTION AND EXCLUSION

The same design decisions become more serious when they determine who can remain in public space. Metal dividers, spikes, narrow ledges and uncomfortable surfaces can make lying down or staying for a long time physically impossible.

These designs often appear neutral because they are part of the architecture. They do not directly name the people being controlled. Instead, they remove certain ways of using space and push vulnerable people toward another street, sidewalk or station.

The design may change the appearance of a place, but it does not address why someone needs to sleep or remain there. The person is moved while the larger problem stays in place. Public seating becomes part of a wider question about access, comfort and who public space welcomes.

ABOUT THE SITE

Take A Seat is a photographic study of public seating and hostile design across New York City. The project records benches that appear ordinary at first, but use small design decisions to shape how people sit, rest and remain in public space.

Armrests, dividers, narrow surfaces and uncomfortable materials can guide the body toward one approved position. These choices make lying down difficult and encourage people to leave after a short period of time.

The website moves from invitation, to guidance, and finally to restriction. Together, the photographs ask when a place to sit becomes a tool for managing bodies and deciding who is allowed to stay.
